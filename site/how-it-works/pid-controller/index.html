How a PID Controller Works

Change the controller gain, integral time and derivative time. Watch the P, I and D bars build the valve output and see how the temperature follows each setpoint step, with less or more overshoot.

Live animationAdjustable slidersFormula and example

Quick answer: A PID controller compares the measured value with the setpoint and moves its output to remove the error. The proportional term acts on the size of the error, the integral term on how long it has lasted and the derivative term on how fast it is changing.

What Is a PID Controller?

PID control is the workhorse of process plants. Nearly every flow, pressure, level and temperature loop in a DCS or PLC uses a PID block to drive a control valve, a damper or a drive.

The controller works on the error, the difference between setpoint and process value. Its output is the sum of three actions: proportional, integral and derivative, each set by one tuning parameter.

Good tuning balances speed and stability. Too much gain or too short an integral time makes the loop oscillate, while too little makes it slow and leaves the process away from setpoint for a long time.

PID Controller Working Principle Step by Step

  1. The transmitter measures the process value, here the tank temperature, and sends it to the controller.
  2. The controller calculates the error e = SP − PV every execution cycle.
  3. The P term gives an output change in proportion to the error, so a bigger error moves the valve further.
  4. The I term keeps adding the error over time until it is zero, removing the offset that P alone leaves.
  5. The D term reacts to the rate of change of the PV and brakes the output as the PV approaches setpoint, reducing overshoot.

PID Controller Formula

Output = Kc × (e + (1 ÷ Ti) × ∫e dt + Td × de/dt)
Proportional band PB = 100 ÷ Kc (%)
With a constant error, the I term ramps at Kc × e ÷ Ti per second
Many controllers apply derivative to the PV only, so a setpoint step does not kick the output
Example: Kc 2, Ti 8 s, Td 1 s, e = 10 %: P = 2 × 10 = 20 %, I ramps at 2 × 10 ÷ 8 = 2.5 % per s, D for a PV rising 1 % per s = −2 × 1 × 1 = −2 %, PB = 50 %

Main Parts of a PID Controller

PartWhat it does
Process value inputMeasurement from the transmitter, scaled to engineering units or percent
SetpointTarget value entered by the operator or sent by another controller
PID algorithmCalculates the P, I and D terms every execution cycle
Output and limitsSends 0 to 100 % to the final element and stops the I term winding up at the limits
Final control elementControl valve, damper or drive that changes the process

Where a PID Controller Is Used

Temperature controlSteam or fuel valves on heat exchangers, furnaces and reactors.
Flow controlFast loops on feeds and utilities, usually PI with little or no derivative.
Pressure controlVessel, header and compressor pressure loops.
Level controlTank and drum levels, often with gentle tuning to smooth the outflow.

Advantages

  • Works on most process loops with only three settings
  • Integral action removes steady offset
  • Well understood, available in every DCS and PLC

Limitations

  • Needs tuning, and the best settings change with the process
  • Derivative amplifies measurement noise
  • Large dead time limits how fast any PID loop can respond

PID Controller Field Tips and Common Problems

  • Check the controller form and units first; gain or proportional band and repeats or minutes per repeat change the meaning of the numbers.
  • Leave derivative at zero for flow and noisy loops and use it mainly on slow temperature loops.
  • Check the valve for stiction and the transmitter for noise before retuning a loop that cycles.
  • Make one change at a time and record the old values so you can return to them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does each term of a PID controller do?

P reacts to the present error, I to the accumulated past error and D to the rate of change, which predicts where the error is heading.

Why does a P only controller leave an offset?

With P alone the output only changes when there is an error, so the loop settles where a small error is needed to hold the required output. Integral action removes it.

What is integral windup?

When the output is at a limit and the error stays, the integral term keeps growing. When the error reverses, the output stays at the limit too long and the PV overshoots; anti windup logic stops this.

What is the difference between gain and proportional band?

They describe the same thing. Proportional band in percent is 100 divided by the gain, so a gain of 2 equals a band of 50 %.
