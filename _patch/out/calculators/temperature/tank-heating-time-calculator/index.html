Heat Up Time Formulas

t = (m × c ÷ UA) × ln[(P − UA(T₀ − Ta)) ÷ (P − UA(T₁ − Ta))]
No heat loss: t = m × c × (T₁ − T₀) ÷ P

m is the liquid mass, c its specific heat, P the heater power delivered to the liquid, UA the tank heat loss coefficient (U × surface area) and Ta the ambient temperature. The liquid is assumed well mixed and the tank wall heat capacity is ignored; add the steel mass × 0.5 kJ/kg·K to m × c for heavy tanks.

Worked example

2000 kg of water heated from 15 to 60 °C by a 30 kW heater with UA = 20 W/K takes about 3.55 h; without losses it would take 3.49 h, because the loss is under 1 kW.

Key insight: heat loss matters most near the target and on poorly insulated tanks. If the loss at target approaches the heater power, the last few degrees take very long.

Estimating UA and the Real Heater Power

The heat loss coefficient UA is the hardest input to know. A simple way to measure it is a cool down test: switch off the heater at a known temperature, log the liquid temperature for an hour or more, and use the rate of fall. UA equals m × c × (rate of fall) ÷ (tank temperature − ambient temperature), with the rate in K/s and m × c in J/K. For a first estimate, U is about 0.4 to 0.8 W/m²·K for 50 to 100 mm of mineral wool insulation and 10 W/m²·K or more for bare steel in still air.

Also check how much heater power really reaches the liquid. An immersion heater delivers almost all of its rating, but only at nominal voltage: at 90 % voltage it gives about 81 % power, because power varies with the square of the voltage. Steam coils lose duty as the liquid approaches steam temperature, and fouled coils lose more, so a heat up that takes much longer than predicted often points to a coil or steam trap problem rather than a calculation error.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is UA?

The heat loss per degree of temperature difference between the tank and ambient, the sum of U × A over all surfaces. It is in W/K.

Does this work for steam coils?

Yes, enter the coil duty as heater power. Coil duty falls as the liquid approaches steam temperature, so use an average value.

Why is mixing important?

Without agitation the liquid stratifies, and the temperature sensor may read the hot or cold layer.
